Gust-interaction experiments take place in a wind tunnel, but the simulations that
accompany them usually leave the tunnel out: no walls, no gust generator, just an
airfoil meeting an idealized gust in free space. I built a computational
counterpart of the tunnel itself, in which the flow near the test subject is
solved as a viscous, unbounded external flow, while the walls, the inlet flow rate
and the gust generator enter through a separate potential flow that cancels the
flow through the walls and introduces the gust as a distribution of sinks on the
top wall. Superposing the two through a Helmholtz decomposition lets the viscous
solver keep its compact lattice Green's function domain around the vorticity,
while the much smoother wall correction is solved on a coarse grid. Modeling a
tunnel inspired by the Andrew Fejer Unsteady Wind Tunnel, I find that the walls
barely matter for a weak gust at zero angle of attack, but that confinement and
the concentrated suction become important at a higher angle of attack or gust
strength — enough to separate the flow where the free-space model keeps it
attached. Because the model is a digital twin of an actual tunnel, it can also be
paired with experiments through data assimilation, which I demonstrate by
inferring the gust strength from noisy synthetic velocity measurements.
